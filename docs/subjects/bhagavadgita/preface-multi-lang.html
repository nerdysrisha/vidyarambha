Before you begin
The place · the people · how to read

The Place

Picture a wide open field in ancient India. This is Kurukshetra, and two enormous armies stand facing each other, about to begin a war. In the quiet space between them, a conversation takes place. That conversation is the Bhagavad Gita: about 700 verses in 18 chapters, found inside the great epic, the Mahabharata.

The Quarrel

Two branches of one royal family are fighting over who should rule the kingdom of Hastinapura: the Pandavas, the five sons of Pandu, and the Kauravas, the hundred sons of Dhritarashtra, led by Duryodhana. Years of broken promises and failed talks have brought them here. Both armies are full of cousins, teachers and friends.

Who's Who

  • ArjunaA Pandava prince and master archer. He arrives ready to fight, then sees his own teachers, elders and relatives standing on the other side. His hands tremble and he puts down his bow. He is every one of us: a good person caught in a choice that hurts.
  • KrishnaArjuna's cousin and close friend, who has agreed to drive his chariot and not to fight. Tradition honours him as divine. In the Gita he is the teacher who answers Arjuna's questions.
  • DhritarashtraThe blind old king of the Kauravas. He is not on the field; he waits in the palace and asks the question that opens the Gita: what happened at Kurukshetra?
  • SanjayaThe king's minister and charioteer, gifted with the power to see distant events. He tells Dhritarashtra everything he sees and hears, and so the Gita reaches us through his voice.
  • Bhishma and DronaThe grand elder and Arjuna's own archery teacher, deeply loved and respected by him, yet standing on the opposing side. Seeing them is what shakes Arjuna most.

Why a Battlefield?

The battlefield is real, but it is also a picture of something every person faces: a moment when duty, love, fear and doubt pull in different directions and no choice feels clean. Arjuna's question, what is right for me to do now?, is not only a warrior's question. It is yours as well. The Gita is the answer he receives, not as a sermon, but as a conversation between two friends.

How to Read

Come with a clean, open mind. For the time you read, set down whatever you already believe or have been told: your faith, your doubts, your labels. Simply listen, the way Arjuna did.

The Gita does not ask you to join any religion or to accept anything in advance. It speaks to the human being in each of us, and so it belongs to all of humanity, whatever your background, and whether you follow a faith or none.

Read slowly and question what you read. Krishna himself closes the conversation by asking Arjuna to reflect on everything fully and then choose freely. Take what helps, and test it in your own life.
